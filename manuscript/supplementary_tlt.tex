\section{Additional Robustness Checks}
\label{sec:s4}

Three further sensitivity analyses complement the robustness results in the main text. First, a complete-case analysis (restricting to the 6.47\% of students with data on all 33 predictors) reduced the unweighted low-performer rate from 45.54\% to 37.88\%, confirming that complete-case analysis would substantially alter the analyzed population and supporting the imputation choice. Second, per-plausible-value label sensitivity: recomputing the low-performer label for each of the ten mathematics plausible values yielded low-performer rates ranging from 45.24\% to 45.34\% (a spread of 0.10 percentage points), within 0.30 percentage points of the mean-plausible-value label (45.54\%), indicating that the modeling outcome is not sensitive to the choice of plausible value. Third, temporal explanation stability: SHAP-based feature-importance rankings computed on PISA 2018 mathematics data were stable relative to PISA 2022---66.7\% of features changed rank by at most one position and 88.9\% by at most two, and HOMEPOS remained the top-ranked predictor in both cycles.

\section{Operational Threshold, Disparate-Impact, and Local Case Analyses}
\label{sec:s7}

Formal fairness metrics are threshold-dependent, so we examine the operating characteristics of the tuned model at the default 0.50 threshold and at the per-subgroup Youden operating point (Table~\ref{tab:supp-threshold}). At the single 0.50 threshold, operating characteristics differ markedly across groups: low-SES students are flagged at a true-positive rate of 0.937 (with a false-positive rate of 0.491), whereas high-SES students are flagged at only 0.393 true-positive (0.046 false-positive)---the model effectively searches harder among low-SES students, consistent with their higher low-performer base rate. Immigrant-background students are flagged at 0.626 true-positive, notably below native students, echoing the under-flagging implied by their subgroup calibration. Moving to a subgroup-specific Youden threshold rebalances true- and false-positive rates (e.g., low-SES non-native: TPR 0.728$\to$0.691, FPR 0.375$\to$0.235), illustrating the accuracy--fairness operating-point trade-off a deployment must choose deliberately.

Disparate-impact screening (the ``4/5ths'' rule~\cite{feldman2015disparate}: an unprivileged selection rate below 80\% of the privileged rate signals adverse impact) applied to the early-warning selection at the 0.50 threshold gives a ratio of 0.61 for immigrant-background versus native students and 0.13 for highest- versus lowest-ESCS students. The immigrant ratio is an equity concern: immigrant-background students have a \emph{higher} low-performer rate than native students yet are flagged \emph{less}, consistent with the subgroup overconfidence documented in Section~S6. The ESCS ratio is prevalence-driven and points the other way---low-SES students are flagged far more often, which is the intended targeting of an equity-aware early-warning system; it illustrates that a raw 4/5ths screen must be read together with base rates and the selection context (support targeting rather than benefit allocation).

\section{A Reusable Fairness-Audit Protocol}
\label{sec:s8}

The evaluation design used here can be packaged as a reusable protocol for auditing predictive models in education:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Outcome and reference group}: fix the outcome definition (e.g., low-performer label) and the privileged/unprivileged reference groups before any model fit.
\item \emph{Primary metric}: use a base-rate-independent ranking metric (ABROCA here) as the operative signal, and report prevalence-sensitive metrics (DP, EO TPR/FPR gaps, F1 gradient) transparently alongside it.
\item \emph{Uncertainty}: report bootstrap confidence intervals on every headline gap (including intersectional gaps), and state whether resampling respects the clustered sampling design.
\item \emph{Calibration parity}: check calibration separately within each protected group (ECE, slope), not only globally.
\item \emph{Intersectional screen}: audit the pairwise (and, where sample size permits, higher-order) intersections of protected attributes, not only each axis alone.
\item \emph{Operating point}: document the threshold and its per-subgroup operating characteristics, and screen the deployment selection with the 4/5ths rule alongside base rates.
\item \emph{Reporting}: publish the per-subgroup metrics, calibration, and operating-point tables in a fixed template (as in this supplementary) so that audits are comparable across contexts and cycles.
\end{enumerate}
A longitudinal extension of this protocol---repeating the audit on PISA 2018 to quantify fairness drift---is feasible; the 2022 cycle's post-pandemic context makes such drift hard to interpret causally, and we leave a formal drift decomposition as future work.

\section{Additional Local Explanation Case Studies}
\label{sec:s9}

Two additional holdout cases complement the borderline case discussed in the main text. First, a correctly flagged student from the low-SES non-native intersection (Dominican Republic, first-generation immigrant, ESCS = $-3.82$) is assigned a low-performer probability of 0.999 and does score 285.6, well below the threshold: the model reliably identifies the most materially disadvantaged students. Second, a high-SES native student (Jordan, ESCS = 0.60) is assigned a probability of 0.961 yet scores 445.4 and is not a low performer: the model can misfire toward over-flagging even for advantaged students, underscoring the value of human review rather than automated denial of support. Both cases are presented as illustrations of fitted-model behavior on the holdout partition, not as evidence about individual students or their circumstances.
