\begin{abstract}
Learning analytics increasingly relies on machine learning to predict student outcomes, but predictive models inform educational practice and learning-technology design only when their decisions can be explained and their impact on different student populations can be audited for fairness. Using PISA 2022 mathematics data for 613,744 students across 80 countries and economies, we develop an explainable, fairness-aware analytical framework. Predictors are organized by Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory and van Dijk's ICT access--skills--usage taxonomy and modeled with tuned XGBoost ensembles reaching AUC = 0.903 for low-performer classification (Brier = 0.126) and a 23\% RMSE reduction over the default-parameter ridge baseline. Explanations synthesize four XAI methods (SHAP, permutation importance, accumulated local effects, and LIME) with rank-correlation validation of cross-method convergence. Formal fairness metrics (Equalized Odds, Demographic Parity, ABROCA) with intersectional subgroup analysis evaluate equity across gender, immigrant background, and socioeconomic status; eight robustness checks assess stability. Individual-level factors dominate prediction, and ICT self-efficacy outweighs simple device availability, though school ICT resources (an access-dimension variable) remain the single strongest ICT predictor overall, supporting a qualified skills-over-access account of digital divides. Socioeconomic status is the largest fairness concern (ABROCA = 0.027), with low-SES immigrant-background students the most underserved subgroup (AUC 0.779 vs.\ 0.880, gap 0.101), worse than either single axis. Cross-country generalization is limited (country-group holdout AUC 0.847), indicating context-specific validation before deployment. We contribute a multi-method XAI comparison and an intersectional fairness audit as a reproducible template for large-scale educational assessment, with implications for early-warning systems, personalized dashboards, and ICT-supported instruction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{L}{earning} analytics and educational data mining increasingly apply machine learning to predict student outcomes from large-scale assessment data. Predictive models can support early-warning systems, personalized dashboards, and adaptive instructional design~\cite{Romero_Ventura_2020}. Tree-based ensembles capture nonlinear predictive structure that linear models miss~\cite{Breiman_2001,Chen_Guestrin_2016}, while post-hoc explanations such as SHAP reveal which variables fitted models rely upon~\cite{Lundberg_Lee_2017}. Yet two requirements must be satisfied before such models can inform educational practice. First, predictions must be interpretable: educators need to understand what drives an early-warning signal, not merely that it fired~\cite{Rudin_2019,Molnar_2022}. Second, predictions must be equitable: models trained on diverse student populations can perpetuate or amplify existing disparities if their performance differs systematically across gender, socioeconomic status, or immigrant background~\cite{Baker_Hawn_2022,Kizilcec_Lee_2022}. The stakes are not abstract: an early-warning model that systematically misjudges low-SES immigrant-background students would concentrate its errors on the very students whose support needs are greatest, and deployment across international assessments would make such a failure global rather than local.

Satisfying these requirements at the scale of international assessment is challenging. PISA 2022, conducted in the aftermath of COVID-19-related school disruptions, provides mathematics performance and detailed ICT-related questionnaire variables for 613,744 students across 80 countries and economies. The predictors of mathematics achievement are not merely statistical variables but theoretically grounded constructs whose relationships embody domain knowledge from educational psychology and digital inequality research. Recent work in this journal demonstrates that machine learning on PISA data can decode contextual factors distinguishing performance levels~\cite{hu2023decoding}. However, existing studies typically rely on a single explanation method, treat fairness as an afterthought, or restrict analysis to one country or region, so the empirical questions of whether multi-method explanations converge and whether formal fairness audits reveal disadvantages hidden by single-axis evaluation on global-scale assessment data remain open.

We address these gaps by developing an explainable, fairness-aware analytical framework for large-scale educational assessment. Predictors are organized using Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory~\cite{Bronfenbrenner_1979,Bronfenbrenner_1994} and van Dijk's ICT access--skills--usage taxonomy~\cite{van_Dijk_2005,van_Dijk_2020}, then modeled with tuned XGBoost ensembles. Explanations are synthesized from four XAI methods with rank-correlation validation of cross-method convergence. Formal fairness metrics and intersectional subgroup analysis evaluate equity across gender, immigrant background, and socioeconomic status. Eight robustness checks assess stability across countries, subgroups, and plausible-value specifications.

The present study addresses four research questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1.} Which features are most predictive of PISA 2022 mathematics achievement when organized under a theory-driven feature framework?
\item \textbf{RQ2.} Do tuned tree-based ensembles outperform linear baselines by a meaningful margin on global-scale educational data?
\item \textbf{RQ3.} Do multiple XAI methods produce convergent feature-importance evidence, and does this convergence strengthen the reliability of model explanations for educational practice?
\item \textbf{RQ4.} Are predictive performance and model explanations stable and equitable across demographic subgroups, socioeconomic strata, and country contexts?
\end{itemize}

This work contributes to learning analytics and educational data mining in three ways.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A systematic multi-method XAI comparison for large-scale educational assessment} (RQ3), showing that SHAP and permutation importance converge ($\rho$ = 0.81), that SHAP is highly consistent across model architectures ($\rho$ = 0.99), and that ALE agrees on the dominant predictors while LIME's global aggregation diverges under feature correlation---providing method-selection guidance for correlated survey data.
\item \textbf{An intersectional fairness audit} (RQ4) showing that formal fairness metrics reveal population segments underserved by predictive models, and that the low-SES non-native immigrant intersection (AUC 0.779 vs.\ 0.880) is markedly worse than either single axis---evidence that single-axis evaluation can conceal compounded disadvantage.
\item \textbf{A reproducible analytical template} (RQ1/RQ2), with the full pipeline operating on a single workstation with fixed random seeds, applied to the complete 80-country PISA 2022 sample.
\end{enumerate}
These contributions carry direct implications for the design of early-warning systems~\cite{baneres2023dropout}, personalized learning dashboards, and ICT-supported instruction---tools at the core of this journal's scope. The rest of this article is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work; Section III describes the data, theoretical framework, and evaluation methodology; Section IV presents results; Section V discusses implications and design guidance; Section VI states limitations; and Section VII concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Explainable Machine Learning in Education}
Gradient boosting models including XGBoost and LightGBM have become standard tools in educational data mining~\cite{Chen_Guestrin_2016,Ke_2017}. Recent systematic and umbrella reviews identify insufficient attention to explainability and fairness as core design objectives~\cite{Wang_2024_AIED_review,pan2025survey,gunasekara2024umbrella}. PISA-based machine learning studies have applied these methods with SHAP interpretation to East Asian systems~\cite{Huang_2024,djekourmane2026eastasia}, Spanish students~\cite{Gomez_Talal_2025}, U.S.\ data~\cite{Zhu_2025_US_PISA}, Latin American low-performer identification~\cite{PISA_2022_XAI_low_performers}, and cross-national samples~\cite{arxiv2025pisa2018}; \"{O}z and Bulut demonstrated ensemble advantages on the full 80-country sample without XAI or fairness evaluation~\cite{Oz_2025_stacking}, and \'Alvarez-Garc\'ia et al.\ applied explainable cluster analysis to PISA 2022~\cite{ce2024profiles}. Within this journal, Hu et al.\ decoded contextual factors of digital reading performance using PISA 2015/2018 data and machine learning~\cite{hu2023decoding}, Lu et al.\ proposed a trustworthy knowledge tracing model for intelligent tutoring systems~\cite{lu2024trustworthy}, and Parsaeifard et al.\ applied explainable machine learning to disengagement detection in distance education~\cite{parsaeifard2026disengagement}.

Because different interpretability algorithms produce complementary but divergent rankings~\cite{Niu_2025} and correlated predictors can distort marginal feature effect estimates~\cite{Herbinger_2024_GADGET}, multi-method comparison is advisable. We compare SHAP, permutation importance, accumulated local effects (ALE)~\cite{Apley_Zhu_2020}, and LIME~\cite{Ribeiro_2016} with rank-correlation validation. The closest study to ours is \'Alvarez-Garc\'ia et al.~\cite{ce2024profiles}, who apply explainable methods to PISA 2022 but rely on cluster analysis rather than supervised prediction, without a formal fairness audit or cross-method convergence validation. Unlike single-method SHAP studies~\cite{Gomez_Talal_2025,PISA_2022_XAI_low_performers}, we validate convergence across four explanation methods, and unlike prior cross-national work on PISA 2018~\cite{arxiv2025pisa2018}, we use the most recent PISA 2022 cycle. This multi-method comparison provides a methodological template for trustworthy explanation in education.

\subsection{Algorithmic Fairness in Educational Prediction}
Predictive models can perpetuate inequities when fairness is not explicitly evaluated~\cite{Baker_Hawn_2022,Kizilcec_Lee_2022}. Sha et al.\ evaluated class-balancing techniques to alleviate algorithmic bias in educational predictive tasks~\cite{sha2022balancing}, and Deho et al.\ assessed how dataset drift degrades the fairness of learning analytics models~\cite{deho2024drift}. Recent work recommends expanding fairness evaluation beyond gender and race to socioeconomic and intersectional subgroups~\cite{Idowu_2024_debiasing}; Mirsch et al.\ demonstrated that intersectional analysis reveals disparities invisible to single-axis evaluation~\cite{Mirsch_2025_intersectional}, and Deho et al.\ examined whether sensitive attributes should be included in learning analytics models at all~\cite{deho2023sensitive}. Systematic mappings likewise identify fairness as a central design concern for educational machine learning~\cite{pham2025fairness}, and ensemble-learning analyses have surfaced ethnic bias in educational text~\cite{albuquerque2026ethnicbias}. Closest to our combination of objectives, Kesgin et al.\ combined explainability and fairness evaluation in student performance prediction~\cite{appl2025fairness}. We extend this line by applying formal fairness metrics (Equalized Odds, Demographic Parity, and the Area Between the ROC Curves, ABROCA~\cite{gardner2019slicing,Li_2025}) with intersectional subgroup analysis to global-scale PISA data. Whereas prior work combining XAI and fairness has focused on single datasets~\cite{appl2025fairness}, we audit equity across the full 80-country sample with intersectional subgroup analysis and eight robustness checks.

\subsection{Research Gap}
Several gaps remain, each addressed by this study. Most PISA-based machine-learning studies rely on single-country samples; we use the full 80-country sample with proper survey-weight and plausible-value conventions~\cite{Rutkowski_2010,OECD_2022_technical}. Multi-method XAI comparisons exist in educational data mining more broadly~\cite{Niu_2025}, and XAI has been combined with fairness evaluation in single-dataset settings~\cite{appl2025fairness}. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, no prior study has combined a multi-method XAI comparison, a formal intersectional fairness audit, and cross-national scope within a single framework on large-scale international assessment data. Formal fairness metrics---particularly intersectional subgroup analysis---remain largely absent from PISA-based machine-learning studies. ICT variables are typically treated as supplementary covariates rather than central analytical targets. Finally, cross-context generalizability of PISA-based models is underexamined~\cite{kleter2024boundary}.

Table~\ref{tab:positioning} positions this study against the closest PISA-based machine-learning work on country scope, model family, XAI coverage, and fairness evaluation. The two features that jointly distinguish this work---multi-method XAI convergence validation and a formal intersectional fairness audit on the full global sample---are absent from every listed study. Studies reporting higher single-model AUC (e.g., G\'omez-Talal et al.~\cite{Gomez_Talal_2025}, 0.977) do so on a single country, where a model faces none of the cross-context heterogeneity that the 80-economy global model must absorb; the comparison is therefore between a country-specific ceiling and a context-generalized estimate, not two estimates of the same quantity.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Data and Feature Organization}
The analytical framework draws on public-use PISA 2022 student and school questionnaire data~\cite{OECD_2022_database}. The processed analysis frame comprises 613,744 students from 80 countries and economies (Table~\ref{tab:sample}; population-weighted descriptive estimates in Table~\ref{tab:descriptive}). Predictors are organized across four ecological levels (individual, interaction, institutional, contextual) following Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory~\cite{Bronfenbrenner_1979,Bronfenbrenner_1994}, and ICT variables are classified by van Dijk's access--skills--usage taxonomy~\cite{van_Dijk_2005,van_Dijk_2020} (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}). This organization operationalizes Bronfenbrenner's process--person--context--time (PPCT) model, a framework with established precedent in learning-analytics research~\cite{tan2017ecological,tong2024bioecological,bond2023engagement}: \emph{process} variables capture the behaviors and interactions most proximal to learning (e.g., mathematics self-efficacy, subject-related ICT use); \emph{person} variables characterize the learner (demographics, immigrant background, self-concept); \emph{context} spans ecological levels from the microsystem (classroom, school) to the macrosystem (country); and \emph{time} is represented by the PISA 2022 cycle, collected following the pandemic-related school disruptions. This mapping makes the theory a structuring device for variable selection and interpretation rather than a post-hoc label. Of 36 configured predictors, 33 were retained with missingness at or below 50\%. These operationalize nine theoretically defined groups---five individual-level construct groups (home and SES, self-beliefs, belonging and safety, demographics, teaching and cognitive), a school climate and resources group, and the ICT access, skills, and usage groups---with each predictor's assignment, construct, and missingness enumerated in the supplementary materials (Section~S5). Among the individual-level predictors are two mathematics self-efficacy scales (MATHEFF and MATHEF21) alongside related self-report measures; because these are collected in the same assessment session as the mathematics outcome, we read their predictive strength as concurrent association and return to the reverse-causality caveat in the Discussion. Missing values were handled by median imputation (numeric) and most-frequent imputation (categorical). Only 6.47\% of students had complete data on all 33 predictors, so complete-case analysis would substantially change the analyzed population. This theory-driven organization yields six testable predictions about expected fitted-model behavior (P1--P6, derived from the ecological levels and the ICT taxonomy), which we verify against the empirical results in Section~V-B.

\subsection{Outcomes}
The continuous outcome is mathematics literacy, operationalized as the row-wise mean of the ten plausible values, a standard convention for PISA secondary analysis that avoids arbitrary single-draw variance:
\begin{equation}
\bar{Y}_i \;=\; \frac{1}{10}\sum_{v=1}^{10} Y_{iv},
\label{eq:pvmean}
\end{equation}
where $Y_{iv}$ is the $v$-th mathematics plausible value of student $i$. The binary outcome is low-performing student status,
\begin{equation}
L_i \;=\; \mathbb{1}[\bar{Y}_i < 420.07],
\label{eq:lowperf}
\end{equation}
where 420.07 is the lower bound of PISA proficiency Level 2, the international baseline-competence threshold that marks the population early-warning and dashboard interventions typically target~\cite{NCES_2022_cut_scores}.

\subsection{Model Training and Evaluation}
For score prediction we report the weighted root mean squared error
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMSE} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{\sum_i w_i (\hat{y}_i - y_i)^2}{\sum_i w_i}},
\label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}
where $w_i$ are normalized student weights and $\hat{y}_i$ the predicted score, and for low-performer classification the area under the ROC curve (AUC) and the Brier score. Baselines comprised ridge regression, elastic net, and L2-regularized logistic regression. ML models included random forest, histogram gradient boosting, XGBoost, and LightGBM, all trained with a global random seed of 20260510. Hyperparameters for XGBoost and LightGBM were tuned using Bayesian optimization (Optuna, 50 trials per model-task, optimizing AUC for classification and RMSE for regression; Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}, with the full search ranges in Supplementary Section~S10). The primary evaluation used a stratified 80/20 holdout with bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals from 1,000 resamples. A supplementary 5-fold cross-validation with default-parameter models (unweighted, as a partition-sensitivity check) confirmed negligible partition sensitivity. Headline results and formal fairness metrics are population-weighted by normalized student weights (W\_FSTUWT, divided by its sample mean so that weights have unit mean), and all compared models---baselines, tuned ensembles, and the stacking variant---are fit under the same normalized weights, so the weighting scheme is held fixed throughout model comparison; cross-validation and explanation-stability checks are unweighted, and this distinction is stated wherever it matters.

\subsection{Multi-Method XAI}
TreeSHAP was computed on a deterministic 5,000-row sample (seed 20260510); explanation quality was verified by reconstructing model output from the SHAP attributions (fidelity R$^2$ = 0.9999 for classification, 1.0000 for regression) and by confirming that the features SHAP ranks as influential also drive prediction change when perturbed (faithfulness $r$ = 0.991 and 0.987)~\cite{bodria2023benchmark}, values characteristic of high-fidelity tree-based SHAP explanations. Permutation importance used a deterministic 10,000-row sample with five repeats. First-order ALE was computed on a 5,000-row sample with 20 bins. LIME produced local explanations on a 500-instance sample as a supplementary comparison; because LIME is a local method, its per-instance attributions were aggregated into a global importance ranking for comparability with the other three methods. All explanation samples are drawn deterministically from the analysis frame under seed 20260510, with the LIME sample a subset of the SHAP sample. Feature-importance rankings from all methods were compared using Spearman's rank correlation.

\subsection{Fairness Evaluation}
Formal fairness metrics were computed for the best classification model across gender, immigrant background, and ESCS quartiles, all weighted by normalized student weights. Let $G \in \{p, u\}$ denote a privileged ($p$) and an unprivileged ($u$) group and $\widehat{L}_i$ the predicted low-performer label at the 0.50 threshold. The Equalized Odds Difference~\cite{Hardt_2016} compares the groups' error rates (Eq.~\eqref{eq:eo}),
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{EO}} = \max\{|\Delta_{\mathrm{TPR}}|, |\Delta_{\mathrm{FPR}}|\},
\label{eq:eo}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_{\mathrm{TPR}} = \mathrm{TPR}_p - \mathrm{TPR}_u$ and $\Delta_{\mathrm{FPR}} = \mathrm{FPR}_p - \mathrm{FPR}_u$ are the signed differences in true- and false-positive rates at the 0.50 threshold (whose absolute values enter Eq.~\eqref{eq:eo}), with $\mathrm{TPR}_g = P(\widehat{L}=1 \mid L=1, G=g)$ and $\mathrm{FPR}_g = P(\widehat{L}=1 \mid L=0, G=g)$. The Demographic Parity Difference~\cite{Dwork_2012} is (Eq.~\eqref{eq:dp})
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{DP}} = \left| P(\widehat{L}=1 \mid G=p) - P(\widehat{L}=1 \mid G=u) \right|,
\label{eq:dp}
\end{equation}
and ABROCA (Area Between the ROC Curves)~\cite{gardner2019slicing} is (Eq.~\eqref{eq:abroca})
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ABROCA} = \int_0^1 \left| \mathrm{ROC}_p(t) - \mathrm{ROC}_u(t) \right| \, dt,
\label{eq:abroca}
\end{equation}
the area between the two groups' ROC curves over the false-positive-rate range $t$; unlike $|\mathrm{AUC}_p - \mathrm{AUC}_u|$, it is positive even when the curves cross. Reference groups were female (vs.\ male), native (vs.\ first- or second-generation immigrant), and the highest-ESCS quartile (vs.\ lowest). We interpret ABROCA as a screening signal for which subgroups deserve closer review in a deployment audit: values below 0.01 are considered negligible, 0.01--0.03 modest but noteworthy, and above 0.03 potentially actionable~\cite{Gandara_2024_blackbox,Verger_2024_MADD}. Because a well-calibrated overall model can still be miscalibrated within subgroups, we additionally report calibration separately by protected group, quantified by the expected calibration error (ECE) over ten equal-width probability bins,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ECE} \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{10} \frac{w^{(k)}}{\sum_j w^{(j)}} \left| \bar{p}^{(k)} - \bar{o}^{(k)} \right|,
\label{eq:ece}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{p}^{(k)}$ and $\bar{o}^{(k)}$ are the weighted mean predicted probability and observed low-performer rate within bin $k$, and by the calibration slope from a logistic regression of observed outcomes on the logit of predicted probabilities; a slope below 1 indicates overconfidence. Intersectional subgroup analysis then examined pairwise combinations of gender, immigrant background, and ESCS quartile (minimum subgroup size 200; group-specific normalized weights), identifying the least-served combined subgroups. Because evaluating each protected axis alone can mask compound disadvantage~\cite{Mirsch_2025_intersectional,pham2025fairness}, we additionally audited the merged low-SES (Q1) non-native immigrant group---the intersection implied by the single-axis results---against the high-SES native reference; e.g., low-SES students with non-native immigrant backgrounds.

\subsection{Robustness Evaluation}
Eight robustness checks were implemented: (1) OECD-only holdout, testing generalization to higher-income contexts; (2) subgroup holdout by gender, immigrant background, and ESCS quintiles, testing equity of the global model; (3) complete-case sensitivity, guarding against imputation dependence; (4) per-plausible-value label sensitivity, guarding against outcome-measurement uncertainty; (5) country fixed-effect comparison; (6) country-group holdout (train 64, evaluate 16), testing transfer to unseen countries; (7) explanation stability across subgroups; (8) temporal explanation stability versus PISA 2018, testing whether interpretations persist across assessment cycles. Checks (1), (5), and (6) are reported in Table~\ref{tab:country}, check (2) in Table~\ref{tab:subgroup}, and check (7) in the text and supplementary materials; checks (3), (4), and (8) are reported in the supplementary materials.

\section{Results}
Results are organized by research question: Section~IV-A addresses RQ2 (model performance), Sections~IV-B and~IV-C address RQ1 and RQ3 (feature importance, XAI convergence, and the ICT analysis), and Sections~IV-D through~IV-F address RQ4 (fairness, cross-context generalization, and subgroup holdout evaluation).
\subsection{Model Performance}
The tuned XGBoost achieved weighted RMSE = 54.10 (R$^2$ = 0.681; 95\% bootstrap CI [53.58, 54.66]) for score prediction and AUC = 0.903 (Brier = 0.126; 95\% bootstrap CI [0.898, 0.907]) for low-performer classification, a 23\% RMSE reduction over the default-parameter ridge baseline (RMSE = 70.13). Tuned LightGBM performed comparably (RMSE = 54.15, AUC = 0.902). The two tuned ensembles are statistically indistinguishable on classification: a paired bootstrap of the holdout AUC difference yields a 95\% interval that contains zero ([$-0.0008$, 0.0013]), so designating XGBoost as the best model is a selection among statistically equivalent candidates rather than a test-set-driven choice. A three-learner stacking ensemble underperformed the best single model (RMSE = 57.07)~\cite{Oz_2025_stacking}. A 3-layer multilayer perceptron baseline, trained on the same weighted data, underperformed the tree ensembles on both tasks (AUC = 0.867, RMSE = 61.68), consistent with the advantage of tree-based ensembles for tabular educational survey data. The best classification model was well-calibrated (calibration slope 0.987, ECE 0.008; Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}, with ECE defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ece}). Supplementary five-fold cross-validation on the default-parameter XGBoost confirmed negligible partition sensitivity (AUC = 0.890 $\pm$ 0.001). Table~\ref{tab:regression} reports regression performance; Table~\ref{tab:classification} reports classification performance.

\subsection{Feature Importance and XAI Convergence}
SHAP and permutation importance both identified home possessions (HOMEPOS) and mathematics self-efficacy (MATHEFF) as the two strongest predictors, with individual-level features dominating (Fig.~\ref{fig:shap}). The SHAP summary is shown for the default-parameter LightGBM (AUC = 0.890), the same model underlying the per-country and country-heterogeneity analyses, so the global-importance evidence rests on a consistent model base; the tuned model (AUC = 0.903) yields the same dominant predictors (cross-model SHAP $\rho$ = 0.99). Cross-model SHAP consistency was very high (XGBoost vs.\ LightGBM, Spearman's $\rho$ = 0.99), and SHAP and permutation importance rankings agreed closely ($\rho$ = 0.81). ALE rankings agreed with SHAP at the top---HOMEPOS and MATHEFF both appear in the top three in each ranking---but agreed more moderately overall ($\rho$ = 0.63), reflecting ALE's focus on first-order marginal effects under feature correlation (Fig.~\ref{fig:xai-convergence}). By contrast, LIME---a local explanation method---diverged substantially from the other three when its per-instance attributions were aggregated into a global ranking ($\rho$ = 0.01), reflecting LIME's sensitivity to feature correlation: perturbing one feature while holding others fixed generates unrealistic synthetic instances outside the data manifold. This indicates that LIME's global aggregation is unreliable for correlated survey data, not that local explanations are without value; for global importance assessment in educational survey data, SHAP, permutation importance, and ALE should be preferred.

Aggregating SHAP attributions by the ecological framework makes the theory's proximal-dominance claim (P1) visible at the group level: individual-level groups carry the largest total attribution (home and SES, self-beliefs, belonging and safety, teaching and cognitive, and demographics together dominate), followed by school climate and resources and then by the ICT access and usage groups (Section~S6 of the supplementary materials). Within the ICT groups, the single skill variable (ICTEFFIC) outweighs the device-availability variables (ICTHOME/ICTSCH), consistent with the skills-over-access pattern reported in Section~IV-C. This group-level readout---available only because predictors are organized theoretically---is the form of explanation an institutional data analyst can act on directly (e.g., which data families to prioritize for collection).

\subsection{ICT Analysis}
The ICT taxonomy revealed that ICT self-efficacy (ICTEFFIC) and ICT resources (ICTRES) were the most important digital-learning predictors in both tasks, while ICT information skills (ICTINFO), ICT home availability (ICTHOME), and ICT school availability (ICTSCH) had smaller importance values (Fig.~\ref{fig:ict}). This skills-over-access pattern---ICT self-efficacy outweighing simple device availability---matches second-level digital divide research~\cite{Scheerder_2017,Hargittai_2010} and the patterns reported in recent systematic reviews of PISA-based ICT studies~\cite{kim2026national} and has a direct design implication: ICT-supported instruction should emphasize building student confidence and skills rather than merely providing devices. One caveat is warranted: ICT school resources (ICTRES), an access-dimension variable, is the single strongest ICT predictor overall, so access still matters---the skills-over-access account describes relative emphasis within the model, not the absence of an access effect, and interventions should maintain resource provision while adding confidence-building.

\subsection{Fairness and Equity}
The fairness audit revealed that socioeconomic status is the largest equity concern: ABROCA = 0.027 (ESCS quartiles), larger than gender (0.006) or immigrant background (0.010) (Table~\ref{tab:fairness-metrics}). By standard benchmarks~\cite{Gandara_2024_blackbox,Verger_2024_MADD}, gender disparities are negligible and immigrant-background disparities are modest, while SES-based disparities are the most pronounced, approaching the actionable threshold. Equalized-odds TPR gaps are largest across SES (0.470), reflecting the much lower low-performer base rate among high-SES students rather than threshold calibration alone. Demographic parity, the equalized-odds TPR gap, and the ESCS F1 gradient (Q1 0.905 to Q5 0.460) are all prevalence-sensitive: they scale with how common low performance is within each group, so large values do not by themselves establish differential treatment. We therefore treat ABROCA---which compares the two groups' ROC curves and is not base-rate-dependent---as the operative fairness metric, and report the prevalence-sensitive metrics transparently alongside it. Intersectional analysis compounded this: low-SES students with non-native immigrant backgrounds were the most underserved subgroup (AUC 0.779 vs.\ 0.880, gap 0.101, $n = 2{,}495$ in the holdout). The merged audit pools first- and second-generation low-SES students, so its AUC is computed on the pooled group and differs from the individual cells in Fig.~\ref{fig:intersectional}. The intersection is substantially worse than either single axis alone (low-SES students overall AUC = 0.872; non-native students overall AUC = 0.860), illustrating how a model can appear broadly equitable along each protected axis while concentrating error on the compounded group. The headline gap is not an artifact of the small holdout subgroup: a weighted bootstrap (1,000 resamples, seed 20260510) yields a 95\% confidence interval of [$-0.168$, $-0.046$] for the AUC difference between the low-SES non-native and high-SES native groups, which excludes zero, and the per-group intervals ([0.716, 0.835] vs.\ [0.872, 0.896]) do not overlap. These findings align with and extend recent TLT research on algorithmic bias in education~\cite{sha2022balancing,deho2024drift} by showing that single-axis fairness evaluation can conceal compounded disadvantage that only intersectional analysis reveals.

Subgroup calibration reveals a complementary equity failure that ranking-based metrics alone miss. The calibration slope is near one within gender (0.990 female, 0.983 male), native students (1.012), and the highest-ESCS quartile (0.988), but falls to 0.813 for immigrant-background students and to 0.602 for the low-SES non-native intersection, indicating strong overconfidence: predicted low-performer probabilities are systematically too extreme for the students the intersectional audit identifies as least well served (Section~S6 of the supplementary materials). ECE correspondingly rises from 0.008 overall to 0.062 (immigrant-background) and 0.122 (low-SES non-native), an order-of-magnitude degradation. Calibration parity therefore does not hold at the intersection even though the global model appears well calibrated, which is operationally material because an early-warning system fires on the calibrated risk score at the 0.50 threshold. Supplementary Sections~S7--S9 provide the operating-point and disparate-impact analysis, a reusable fairness-audit protocol, and two further local case studies.

\subsection{Cross-Context Generalization}
The headline AUC of 0.903 is computed on a student-level holdout in which students from the same countries appear in both training and test partitions, so it estimates within-country predictive performance; the country-disjoint holdout below instead estimates generalization to unseen country contexts and is the more relevant estimate for cross-national deployment. The OECD holdout produced AUC = 0.877; adding country fixed effects improved classification AUC from 0.843 to 0.873. The country-group holdout (train 64, evaluate 16) yielded AUC = 0.847, and per-country AUC ranged from 0.71 to 0.94 (Fig.~\ref{fig:country}), confirming that a single global model cannot be assumed to transfer across contexts. This mirrors the boundary-conditions thread in this venue on predictive-model generalizability~\cite{kleter2024boundary}. Because PISA data are nested (students within schools within countries), pooling them under an independence assumption raises the question of how much of the variance is structured by the clustering. An intercept-only mixed-effects decomposition of the mathematics outcome yields intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) of 0.559 at the school level and 0.307 at the country level for score prediction, and 0.412 (school) and 0.232 (country) for the low-performer outcome. Substantial school- and country-level variance is consistent with the macrosystem interpretation above and with the observed country-level generalization boundary; the magnitudes are within the range reported for international large-scale assessments~\cite{brunner2018between}. These ICCs also imply that student-level resampling, as used for the bootstrap intervals reported in this article, may understate uncertainty attributable to higher-level clustering, a caveat we carry into the Limitations. The theoretical interpretation, that macrosystem (country-level) factors shape how lower-level predictors operate, provides a principled explanation for why generalization fails. Table~\ref{tab:country} reports the full cross-context robustness results.

\subsection{Subgroup Holdout Evaluation}
Performance was broadly stable by gender (AUC = 0.901 female, 0.904 male) but declined for immigrant-background students (second-generation AUC = 0.847) and, most notably, for low-SES students: ESCS Q1 students achieved AUC = 0.871 and Q5 students AUC = 0.886, yet F1 dropped sharply across the ESCS gradient (Q1 F1 = 0.905 vs.\ Q5 F1 = 0.460), indicating that the model's precision-recall trade-off differs substantially by socioeconomic context (Table~\ref{tab:subgroup}). Subgroup holdouts use ESCS quintiles, whereas the fairness audit in Section~IV uses ESCS quartiles; the two Q1 estimates are consistent (AUC 0.871 quintile-based vs.\ 0.872 quartile-based). Explanation stability across subgroups was high (pairwise $\rho$ = 0.72--0.99), with the lowest consistency between the most extreme ESCS quintiles.

\section{Discussion}
We first position the headline results against the closest PISA-based machine-learning studies. Direct quantitative comparison is constrained by differences in outcome definitions, weighting schemes, and evaluation protocols: \'Alvarez-Garc\'ia et al.~\cite{ce2024profiles} apply explainable clustering rather than supervised prediction, G\'omez-Talal et al.~\cite{Gomez_Talal_2025} restrict analysis to Spanish subsamples, and \"{O}z and Bulut~\cite{Oz_2025_stacking} evaluate ensembles without XAI or fairness auditing. Within these caveats, the tuned model's AUC of 0.903 is competitive given the 80-economy scope: single-country analyses such as G\'omez-Talal et al.~\cite{Gomez_Talal_2025} report higher AUCs on restricted samples that face no cross-context heterogeneity (Table~\ref{tab:positioning}), and the country-group holdout (0.847) makes the generalization boundary explicit in a way single-country analyses cannot.
\subsection{Explainability for Educational Practice}
Cross-method convergence matters for practice: when multiple explanation methods agree, educators can trust that a reported feature importance reflects a stable property of the fitted model rather than an artifact of one method's assumptions---a property that recent work on explanation stability in learning analytics~\cite{tiukhova_xai_stability} and on explainable disengagement detection in distance education~\cite{parsaeifard2026disengagement} likewise emphasizes. The LIME divergence provides a concrete caution for practitioners building early-warning or dashboard analytics. This supports the pragmatic position adopted here: multi-method post-hoc explanation provides an interpretability bridge when a performance advantage persists, while a glass-box model such as EBM is a natural choice when it does not.

\subsection{From Analytics to Action: An Operational Early-Warning Workflow}
A predictive early-warning system deployed without fairness auditing risks systematically missing the very students most in need: low-SES immigrant-background students---a failure mode consistent with recent learning-analytics evidence on accuracy-fairness trade-offs in student-performance prediction~\cite{opoku2025fairness,cheng2025balancing}. Following the analytics-driven-design paradigm of Mangaroska and Giannakos~\cite{mangaroska2018design} and the deployment-readiness framing of Wise et al.~\cite{wise2018ready}, we translate the findings above into an operational workflow for fairness-aware early-warning systems, in the spirit of embedded prediction systems such as Ba\~neres et al.~\cite{baneres2019early} and the practical recommendations of Rets et al.~\cite{rets2023recommendations}. Operationalizing these indicators requires five deployment steps. (1) \emph{Local validation}: before deployment in a new context, collect per-context validation data---the country-group holdout decline (AUC 0.890 to 0.847) shows that a global model cannot be assumed to transfer. (2) \emph{Inference and calibration}: deploy the model and monitor calibration drift continuously, following recent evidence that calibrated, fairness-audited early-warning systems substantially improve deployment properties~\cite{wu2026calibrated}. (3) \emph{Indicator generation}: produce per-student risk scores together with subgroup and intersectional fairness metrics. (4) \emph{Delivery with human oversight}: dashboards surface flags to instructors, who retain the ability to review, personalize, or override recommendations; the system never denies support automatically. (5) \emph{Logging and feedback}: log intervention outcomes and subgroup performance to enable iterative refinement, closing the loop between analytics and learning design~\cite{mangaroska2018design,rets2023recommendations}.

\subsection{Design Implications for Learning Technologies}
The framework offers several practical advantages. \emph{Reproducibility}: the full pipeline runs on a single workstation (~12 hours, fixed random seeds), lowering replication barriers. \emph{Explanation method selection}: the cross-method comparison provides a template for choosing XAI methods in correlated tabular educational data, applicable beyond PISA to VLE logs, MOOC data, and institutional early-warning datasets. \emph{Fairness-aware dashboard design}: the intersectional audit identifies specific population segments a model serves less well, enabling dashboard designers to surface equity metrics alongside performance metrics. \emph{ICT instruction}: the skills-over-access finding suggests ICT-supported instruction should emphasize confidence-building rather than mere device provision.

\subsection{Educational Re-Reading of the Explanations and Fairness Findings}
The interpretability and equity results acquire practical meaning only when read against instructional practice, and three connections deserve to be made explicit. First, the dominance of home possessions (HOMEPOS) and mathematics self-efficacy (MATHEFF) is a \emph{screening} signal, not an intervention prescription: both predictors are concurrent and themselves shaped by prior achievement and socioeconomic context (Section~VI), so a dashboard should present the risk score alongside the dominant contributors and a prompt to verify, not remediate on the model's word alone. Second, the skills-over-access ICT finding (Section~IV-C) is directly actionable: ICT-supported instruction should budget for confidence-building tasks and digital-skills scaffolding, not only device provision, an emphasis consistent with ICT self-efficacy outweighing the device-availability variables in the layer-level readout (Section~S6). Third, the calibration results define what an equity-aware early-warning dashboard must surface. Consider a holdout student from the low-SES non-native intersection whom the model assigns a risk probability of 0.03 yet who scores 415.5 on the mathematics scale---just below the 420.07 baseline-competence threshold and therefore a true low performer. The model misses this borderline student, and the subgroup calibration slope of 0.60 indicates the model is overconfident precisely for students like this one. An operational dashboard should therefore (i) display the subgroup calibration slope beside the individual risk score, so that an overconfident trigger for a low-SES immigrant-background student carries a visible uncertainty warning, and (ii) route borderline predictions for low-SES non-native students to human review rather than automated action, the joint design consequence of the intersectional AUC gap (Section~IV-D) and the calibration failure.

\section{Limitations}
Several limitations warrant noting. The analysis is cross-sectional and observational: findings identify predictive patterns, not causal effects, and model explanations should be interpreted as fitted-model patterns rather than evidence about underlying causes. This caution applies with particular force to the strongest predictors---mathematics self-efficacy (MATHEFF and MATHEF21) and home possessions---which are concurrent self-report or contextual measures: self-efficacy may partly reflect prior achievement, so predictive strength should not be read as evidence that raising self-efficacy alone raises performance.

The primary model was selected by comparing holdout performance across tuned variants, so the reported bootstrap confidence intervals are selection-conditional; the small margin over the second-best tuned model (AUC 0.903 vs.\ 0.902) and the supplementary 5-fold cross-validation (AUC 0.890 $\pm$ 0.001) mitigate, but do not eliminate, this concern. The intersectional AUC gap carries a bootstrap confidence interval that excludes zero ([$-0.168$, $-0.046$]), but the relevant holdout subgroup is small ($n = 2{,}495$), and the student-level resampling used for the interval does not fully account for the school- and country-level clustering quantified by the ICCs in Section~IV-E, so the precise magnitude should be treated as indicative pending larger intersectional samples.

The operational early-warning workflow is a data-validated design template, not an evaluated intervention; deployment readiness requires pilot evaluation in an institutional setting. Cross-country generalization is limited: the country-group holdout decline (from the default LightGBM global holdout AUC of 0.890 to 0.847 on held-out countries) indicates that a single global model cannot serve all contexts equally, and per-country validation data should be collected before deployment (full per-country metrics in the supplementary materials).

Missing data were handled by imputation rather than model-based approaches; the 6.47\% complete-case rate makes the imputation step a material modeling decision, and a formal ablation comparing organized versus flat feature representations on explanation outcomes remains future work. This single-author study spans educational theory, machine learning, and fairness evaluation; the structured validation protocol (Table~\ref{tab:propositions}) was designed to make domain assumptions explicit and testable. Additional robustness and interpretability details---intersectional fairness metrics, explanation stability across subgroups, additional robustness checks, and the full 80-country performance breakdown---are provided in the supplementary materials.

\section{Conclusion}
This study developed and evaluated a theory-driven, explainable, and fairness-audited analytical framework for large-scale educational assessment, applied to PISA 2022 mathematics data for 613,744 students across 80 countries. Returning to the research questions: (1) individual-level features---particularly home possessions and mathematics self-efficacy---are the strongest predictors. (2) Tuned tree-based ensembles outperform linear baselines by a substantial margin, achieving a 23\% RMSE reduction over the default-parameter ridge baseline. (3) SHAP is highly consistent across model architectures ($\rho$ = 0.99) and with permutation importance ($\rho$ = 0.81), ALE agrees on the dominant predictors ($\rho$ = 0.63), and LIME diverges under feature correlation, serving as a methodological caution. (4) Predictive performance and explanations are broadly stable in AUC across subgroups, with notable declines for immigrant-background (second-generation AUC 0.847) and low-SES (Q1 F1 0.905 vs.\ Q5 0.460) students, while cross-country generalization is limited and socioeconomic disparities constitute the largest fairness concern (ABROCA = 0.027).

The framework makes three contributions: (a) a systematic multi-method XAI comparison demonstrating convergent and divergent evidence patterns for large-scale assessment data; (b) an intersectional fairness audit framework establishing quantitative baselines for equitable deployment; and (c) a reproducible analytical template applicable to PISA 2025, TIMSS, PIRLS, and institutional learning-analytics datasets. The practical implication is not that machine learning should replace traditional educational statistics, but that explainable, fairness-audited analytics can complement them---provided models are interpreted as predictive rather than causal, and cross-context generalization limits are transparently documented. Future work includes a controlled glass-box-versus-ensemble comparison on identical data and weighting, cluster-aware (school- and country-stratified) bootstrap confidence intervals for the intersectional gap that fully account for the nesting quantified in Section~IV-E, and pilot evaluation of the early-warning workflow in an institutional setting.
