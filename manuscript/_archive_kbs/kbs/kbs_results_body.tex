\subsection{Sample and variable audit}

The knowledge acquisition layer processed 613,744 students from 80 countries, organizing 33 predictors across four hierarchical levels (individual, interaction, institutional, contextual), with ICT variables classified by the three-level access--skills--usage taxonomy (Section~2). The processed analytic frame (Table~\ref{tab:sample}) showed pronounced cross-national variation in weighted low-performer rates (below 25\% to above 70\%; Table~\ref{tab:descriptive}), motivating the country fixed-effect and country-group holdout checks.

\subsection{Model performance}

The inference engine (tuned XGBoost) achieved weighted RMSE~=~54.10 (R\textsuperscript{2}~=~0.681) for score prediction and AUC~=~0.903 (Brier~=~0.126) for low-performer classification, a 23\% RMSE reduction over ridge regression (RMSE~=~70.13). Tuned LightGBM performed comparably (RMSE~=~54.15, AUC~=~0.902); full results are reported in Tables~\ref{tab:regression} and~\ref{tab:classification}. A three-learner stacking ensemble underperformed the best single model (RMSE~=~57.07), consistent with prior findings that heterogeneous base learners can dilute meta-learner signal on tabular data.

The knowledge extraction layer's SHAP and permutation importance analyses both identified home possessions (HOMEPOS) and mathematics self-efficacy (MATHEFF) as the two strongest predictors, with individual-level features dominating the hierarchy (Figures~\ref{fig:classification-shap} and~\ref{fig:regression-shap}; SHAP cross-model consistency Spearman's~$\rho$~=~0.83). The prominence of school-level variables (student and teacher behavior, resource shortages) confirms institutional-level predictive contribution beyond individual characteristics, as predicted by the hierarchical feature organization.

\subsection{ICT feature analysis}

The knowledge representation layer's ICT taxonomy made visible that ICTRES and ICTEFFIC were the most important digital-learning predictors in both tasks; ICTSUBJ and STUDYHMW ranked next, while ICTINFO, ICTHOME, and ICTSCH had smaller importance values (Figure~\ref{fig:digital-importance}). This pattern---visible precisely because the ICT taxonomy disaggregates seven ICT variables by theoretical level---matches the taxonomy's prediction that skills and confidence carry greater predictive weight than device access alone.

\subsubsection*{Cross-country generalization}

The OECD holdout evaluation produced AUC~=~0.877 and RMSE~=~58.35. Adding country fixed effects improved classification AUC from 0.843 to 0.873. The country-group holdout (train 64, evaluate 16) yielded AUC~=~0.847 and RMSE~=~65.23---confirming that context-specific validation is warranted before deployment (Table~\ref{tab:country}). Complete-case sensitivity analysis confirmed that only 6.47\% of students had complete data on all 33 predictors (complete-case low-performer rate 37.88\% vs.~45.54\% in the full sample), confirming that complete-case analysis would substantially change the analyzed population.

\subsubsection*{Explanation stability across subgroups}

SHAP-based feature importance rankings showed high cross-group agreement across ESCS quintiles (pairwise $\rho$~=~0.72--0.99, all $p$~$<$~0.001), with the lowest consistency between the most extreme quintiles (Q1 vs.~Q5, $\rho$~=~0.72). Supplementary Table~S4 reports the full pairwise matrix. This indicates that the fitted models relied on a broadly similar predictor set across socioeconomic groups, with modestly divergent patterns at the extremes.

\subsection{ALE analysis and multi-method XAI comparison}

First-order ALE results for the best classification model were closely aligned with SHAP and permutation importance rankings (Spearman's~$\rho$~=~0.76; top-three predictors identical). ICT self-efficacy achieved a higher ALE rank than its SHAP rank, likely reflecting ALE's conditioning approach isolating each ICT dimension's unique contribution under feature correlation \citep{Molnar_2022,Herbinger_2024_GADGET}. By contrast, LIME rankings diverged substantially from the other three methods (Spearman's~$\rho < 0.03$; Supplementary Table~S5), reflecting LIME's sensitivity to feature correlation: perturbing one feature while holding others fixed generates unrealistic synthetic instances outside the data manifold \citep{Molnar_2022,Niu_2025}. For educational survey data, SHAP, permutation importance, and ALE should be preferred over LIME for global importance assessment.

\subsection{Fairness and equity evaluation}

The decision-support layer's fairness audit computed algorithmic fairness metrics for the best classification model across gender, immigrant background, and ESCS quartiles. The TPR gaps were less than 0.01 by gender, 0.04 by immigrant background, and 0.07 between the highest- and lowest-SES quartiles. ABROCA values were 0.008 (gender), 0.015 (immigrant background), and 0.023 (ESCS)---the latter being the largest fairness concern, falling within the 0.01--0.03 range considered modest but noteworthy \citep{Gandara_2024_blackbox,Verger_2024_MADD}. Intersectional analysis revealed compounded disadvantage: the least-served subgroup (lowest ESCS quartile with non-native immigrant background) had AUC~=~0.831 and F1~=~0.712, a gap of 0.072 in AUC and 0.112 in F1 relative to the most-served subgroup. These findings align with calls for intersectional fairness evaluation in educational algorithms \citep{Idowu_2024_debiasing,Verger_2024_MADD}; they remain descriptive of the fitted predictive model and do not constitute causal evidence of algorithmic discrimination.

\subsection{Explanation fidelity and faithfulness}

Following the benchmark of K{\i}sman et al.\ \citep{Kisman_2026} (Fidelity~=~0.95, Faithfulness~=~0.85 for SHAP explanations of PISA mathematics models), the best classification model (tuned XGBoost) achieved Fidelity~=~0.9999 and Faithfulness~=~0.991; the best regression model achieved Fidelity~=~1.000 and Faithfulness~=~0.987. These values confirm that the SHAP explanations presented in Sections~5.3--5.4 reliably represent the underlying model behavior.
